Functional

Circles that exist out of necessity, shaped not for meaning but for what they allow, load carried, traffic flowing, water contained, across wheels, circulation, and what lies underfoot.

Wheels

The History

No wheels exist in nature. Unlike most inventions, which borrow from the natural world, the wheel has no ancestor. It’s entirely, stubbornly human. Evidence indicates it was created as a potter’s wheel around 3500 BCE in Mesopotamia, three hundred years before anyone thought to use it for a chariot. Before it carried anything, it simply spun in place, shaping clay into vessels and bowls. Movement wasn’t the idea yet. Making something was.

Three centuries later, someone laid that spinning disc on its side, fixed it to an axle, and found it could carry weight.

By 3000 BCE, the Sumerians were using solid wooden wheels on carts, they were heavy and slow, but capable of moving loads no human back ever could alone. Around 2000 BCE, the Sintashta culture built the first spoked wheel, which was lighter and faster than anything that had been made before. By 1500 BCE, spoked chariots dominated warfare from the Near East to China. Around 1000 BCE, Celtic chariot makers added iron rims and repositioned wheels for greater stability, refinements that held for nearly three thousand years.

It wasn’t until the 19th century that things moved again. In 1802, the first wire tension spoke was patented, eventually evolving into the thin, taut spokes still used on bicycles today. In 1845, the pneumatic tire followed, air replacing solid material inside the wheel for the first time, refined shortly after by John Dunlop. In 1885, Karl Benz’s Motorwagen, one of the world’s first automobiles, rolled on wire wheels and was wrapped in hard rubber, the same lineage of spokes and rims which stretch back to Sintashta chariots four thousand years earlier.

Wheels that are made today are almost nothing like that first wooden disc, cast or forged from aluminum alloy, poured molten into a mold or pressed under enormous force, then machined down to an exact, balanced shape before a rubber tire, itself a blend of synthetic and natural rubber reinforced with steel or fabric belts, is fitted around the rim. A modern car wheel is manufactured to tolerances which are measured in fractions of a millimeter. They are spun and tested for balance before it ever touches a road. A kind of precision the Sumerians could never have imagined for a shape they first carved by just the hand.

Every wheel that came after still carries the outline of that first spinning disc in Mesopotamia. It just took three hundred years to realize it could also carry us, and another four thousand to make it this exact.

The Unwanted One

For most of history, luggage wasn’t rolled, it was carried, heavy trunks of wood, leather, and iron needing multiple hands to move at all. Only around the turn of the 20th century did lighter, single person suitcases become the standard for society.

It took until 1970 for anyone to put wheels directly onto a suitcase. That year, Bernard Sadow, an American luggage executive, dragged his own bags through customs after a family vacation, went home, and strapped four casters from a wardrobe trunk onto a suitcase. He patented it in 1972, by which point Macy’s was already advertising it all over as “the luggage that glides.”

But Sadow’s version, towed flat on a leash like strap and it took almost two decades to catch on. The real breakthrough came in 1987, when pilot Robert Plath, working out of his own garage, stood the suitcase upright, added two wheels and a collapsible handle, and built the Rollaboard. He made them first for fellow pilots and flight attendants, the public caught on watching flight crews stroll through airports pulling them behind.

Today’s suitcases roll on four or eight wheels and spin a full 360 degrees.

Extension of Self

The earliest evidence of wheeled seating goes back to the sixth century AD, stone carvings from Ancient China and Greece showing people transported on wheeled furniture. In Medieval Europe, wheelbarrows, introduced in the 1200s for building and farming, were repurposed to carry people with limited mobility to church, to markets, into community life they’d otherwise have been shut out of.

One of the best documented early wheelchairs was built in 1595 for Philip II of Spain, plagued by gout in his final years. It was ornate, plush upholstery, armrests, legrests, four small wheels, but he still needed a servant to push it. Mobility, even then, depended on who could afford it. Today’s wheelchair wheels are self propelled and precisely engineered. They are built for a person to move through the world entirely on their own terms.

A Wheel Built to Look at

Before it was a carnival fixture, the Ferris wheel started small, buckets fixed to a wheel, lifting water from a stream, the kind of thing children would grab and ride just for the fun of the spin. It became something else entirely in 1893, when George Washington Gale Ferris Jr. unveiled a 250 foot wheel at Chicago’s World’s Columbian Exposition, capable of carrying 2,160 people at once. The fair marked 400 years since Columbus reached the New World, and organizers wanted a centerpiece to rival the Eiffel Tower, built four years earlier for Paris’s own World’s Fair.

For the first time, a wheel wasn’t carrying weight, or speed, or cargo. It was carrying people purely for the feeling of it.

Circulation

USA’s First Traffic Circle

Long before Columbus Circle became a traffic circle, this spot was Lenape land, then rural countryside absorbed into Manhattan’s grid as the city grew north through the 1800s. In 1857, Central Park claimed the area, and Frederick Law Olmsted folded a circular roundabout into his and Calvert Vaux’s “Greensward Plan” the same design later adopted for the city’s first traffic circle.

By 1868, the space was called “The Circle” and the city commissioned a statue of Christopher Columbus to put at the center. It was unveiled in 1892 on Columbus Day, marking 400 years since his first voyage. He was standing on top of a 70 foot granite column facing Broadway.

Construction of the circle began in 1901 and finished in 1905, it was designed by William P. Eno, who was a pioneer of traffic engineering. Vehicles entering had to yield to traffic already inside, eliminating left turns across oncoming cars and reducing collisions. Before Columbus’s name stuck, it had already been called Grand Circle, Library Circle, and St. Mary’s Circle.

The U.S. Department of Transportation recognizes it as the country’s first traffic circle. In 2013, the Roundabout Appreciation Society named it the world’s best traffic circle and more than a century later, it is still one of Manhattan’s busiest hubs, and is exactly as Eno designed it.

Traffic Engineered

A traffic circle works on one simple rule, yield to what’s already inside. This way you never need to turn left across oncoming traffic. That single decision removes the most dangerous maneuver at any intersection and replaces it with a continuous, one directional flow. Cars first slow down, then they merge, and finally they exit at their own pace.

Grandstands and arenas run on the same underlying logic, just applied to people instead of cars. Curved, tiered seating wraps around a central point so every seat has a clear line of sight, no columns, no dead zones. Madison Square Garden takes this further, its bowl shaped seating and cable supported roof eliminating interior support columns entirely, and it sits directly on top of Penn Station, one circular system of movement layered above another.

None of these spaces are round for beauty. They’re round because a circle is simply the most efficient shape for moving many things around one shared center, without anything colliding.

Underfoot

Why Round?

Manholes date back to the mid 19th century, when cities began building underground sewer systems and needed access points a person could physically climb through to inspect and maintain them. The covers sealing those openings, generally 200 to 300 pounds of concrete or cast iron, keep pedestrians from falling in and keep street debris from clogging the pipes below.

The question of why they’re round became so widely asked that Microsoft used it in job interviews throughout the 1990s, not because there’s one single correct answer, but because it reveals how someone reasons through an open ended problem. There isn’t just one explanation. A round cover can’t fall through its own opening no matter how it’s turned or rotated, unlike a square one, which can slip through diagonally.

It’s also easier to move, it can simply be rolled rather than carried. And a circle is the most material efficient shape for its width, cheaper and simpler to manufacture than a square cover of the same size. Even the holes themselves tend to come out round, since they’re typically cut with rotational tools that naturally produce circular shapes.

Art Beneath your Feet

New York’s oldest known manhole cover sits on a short block of Jersey Street in Soho, stamped “Croton Aqueduct” and dated 1866, a 19th century relic still doing the same job it was cast for over 150 years ago.

Older foundries treated their covers almost like advertisements, ornate, decorative, meant to be noticed. The Farrin & McCullough foundry cast one with a sunflower motif on St. Luke’s Place. Cornell’s Iron Works made one with a delicate floral pattern still visible on Charlton Street. One 19th century craftsman, Jacob Mark, even embedded a six sided star pattern with colored glass into his designs, small windows set directly into cast iron.

Most covers here are anonymous, made by unnamed foundry workers, but a few carry real artistic signatures. In 2000, artist Lawrence Weiner designed 19 manhole covers installed south of Union Square, each reading “In Direct Line with Another & the Next” a quiet reference to the city’s grid. Only two survive today, one by the benches at Union Square, another outside the Whitney Museum.

Even the plainest, most utilitarian covers carry a hidden language. Letters stamped into the metal, ECS for Empire City Subway, IRT and BRT for two long merged subway systems, quietly chronicle overlapping histories, telegraph lines, vanished transit companies, water systems built a century ago and still running beneath the street. A few covers even mark where the city’s oldest waterways once ran. Peer through one on Minetta Street and, if the light is right, you can still glimpse the Minetta Brook, a natural stream long since buried beneath Manhattan, flowing invisibly on exactly as it always has.

Some covers even record where they were made, “Made in U.S.A.” stamped beside “Made in India” small words carrying an entire history of global manufacturing and labor most people will never notice underfoot.

Many, Made One

A circle doesn’t have a front or a back, no head of the table, no best seat. Everyone standing around one is equally close to the center, which is what makes it the natural shape for gathering, no hierarchy built into the geometry itself, no one positioned above anyone else.

The Guggenheim pulls that idea into motion, a spiral instead of a closed loop, but the same logic holds. Visitors circle a single open center as they move upward through the building, the artwork on the walls changing, the core they’re orbiting never does. It’s a gathering that unfolds over time rather than all at once, still built around the same basic idea, which is many people moving around one shared middle.

The Unisphere does something similar at a much larger scale, gathering an idea instead of a crowd. A single sphere meant to represent the entire world at once, every country, every ocean folded into one continuous surface, with no single point on it more important than another.

Neither shape is round because it needed to be, structurally or otherwise. Both are round because a circle is simply what it looks like when many things, people, countries, ideas, are trying to become one single thing.

Gathering

Wheel of Fortune

Long before Pat Sajak ever spun a wheel on television, Fortune already had one of her own. The Wheel of Fortune, or Rota Fortunae, comes from ancient Greek and Roman mythology, the goddess Fortuna spinning her wheel to decide who rises and who falls, with no warning and no logic to it. Both the Roman scholar Cicero and the Greek poet Pindar wrote about her wheel. Centuries later, Chaucer used it in The Canterbury Tales to describe the downfall of once great historical figures, and Shakespeare leaned on it too, in King Lear, a disguised Earl of Kent cries out, “Fortune, good night, smile once more; turn thy wheel!”

So it’s a little fitting that when spin the bottle first was released and got a name in print, in 1927, it wasn’t called a kissing game at all. It was called “Bottle of Fortune.” There was no goddess required, just a glass bottle on a floor and whoever it happened to land on, the same old idea in miniature, chance deciding something you had absolutely no control over. It is such a large part of our culture now, but it has essentially existed forever.

Astrological Circle

The zodiac is a circle because the sky itself moves in one. As Earth orbits the sun, the sun appears to trace a continuous loop against the background stars over a year, a path ancient astronomers called the ecliptic. It has no natural beginning or end. Babylonian astronomers, around 700 BCE, divided that loop into twelve equal separate sections, each one named for the twelve constellations that the sun seemed to pass through. Hence, giving us the word zodiac, from the Greek zoidiakos kyklos, literally translating to “circle of animals.” Dividing the year into twelve wasn’t inventing a shape so much as tracing one that was already there.

Evil Eye

According to Dr. Nese Yildiran, an art history professor at a University in Istanbul, the earliest known eye amulets date back to 3300 BCE, unearthed at Tell Brak, one of the oldest cities in Mesopotamia, in what’s now modern day Syria, abstract alabaster figures carved with wide, incised eyes staring outward. The recognizable cobalt blue glass version most people know today didn’t appear until much later, around 1500 BCE, when it began spreading across the Mediterranean.

Fate and Cycles

Wishing

Bethesda Fountain sits at the heart of Central Park, its bronze angel rising above a basin where people have tossed coins for generations, making a wish they probably couldn’t fully explain. The angel’s name, Angel of the Waters, and a story most people walking past will probably never learn.

The fountain takes its name from a passage in the Gospel of John, describing a pool in Jerusalem where an angel periodically stirred the water, and whoever stepped in first after the stirring would be healed. New York’s fountain wasn’t built to recreate that miracle, though. It was built to celebrate one that had already happened, commemorating the Croton Aqueduct finally bringing clean water to a city devastated by cholera. The angel wasn’t stirring water to heal anyone anymore. She stood over water that had already stopped making people sick.

None of that history is visible in the coin someone tosses over the railing today. They’re just making a wish, the same instinct that’s followed circles of water for thousands of years, coins in, hope out, no guarantee it works, just the simple need to ask for something and let it go.

The Shutter and the Star

In 1903, a synagogue opened on Rivington Street on the Lower East Side, built for a community of Romanian Jews, and later Polish Jews as the neighborhood shifted over the decades. It was called the Erste Warshawer Synagogue. Above the entrance sat a round window, its steel frame bent into a Star of David, a shape that would have been entirely unremarkable at the time, just one more synagogue on a block already full of them.

By the 1970s, the Lower East Side had changed enormously. The Jewish community that once filled that block had largely moved on, to other boroughs and other neighborhoods. The synagogue on Rivington Street sat empty. Its doors were gone entirely. The only sign anyone still claimed the building was a “For Sale” notice out front. People wandered in and out freely, since there was no one left to stop them, some using the space to get high, undisturbed in a building that had once demanded reverence. Rain came straight through what was left of the roof, pooling on floors that had once held rows of worshippers.

Hale Gurland wasn’t looking for any of this. He was simply on his way to Orchard Street to buy a new pair of shoes when he passed the building, noticed the sign, and stepped inside out of curiosity. What he found stopped him completely. “The place looked like Dresden after the bombs. Rain was coming through the roof.” He forgot about the shoes entirely. “It looked like they had a service, and everybody left. It was really cool.”

Gurland was a sculptor, working mostly in large scale metal, the kind of artist who needed real space and wasn’t afraid of a building most people would have walked straight past. He ended up moving in, first as a studio, eventually as his home, the line between the two blurring the way it often does for artists who end up living inside their own work.

Rebuilding a building that had been stripped down to almost nothing meant working through it piece by piece, patching a roof that had been open to the sky for who knows how long, deciding what stayed and what didn’t. At some point in that long process, he reached the round window above the entrance, the one that had held its Star of David since 1903, somehow still intact after nearly everything else in the building had fallen apart around it. He had a choice in front of him, he could take it out entirely or he could restore it carefully, put it back exactly as it once was. He did neither.

Instead, he took the very same steel, the same six points that had marked this building as a place of Jewish worship for seventy years, and bent it into something new entirely, the six points of the star reshaped into the blades of a camera’s shutter, the kind you’d find inside an old mechanical lens, the kind that opens and closes to let exactly the right amount of light through.

That window is still there today, above the same entrance on Rivington Street, same circle, same steel, but carrying two completely different meanings depending on which decade you happen to be standing in. First it marked a place of prayer, something a whole community gathered beneath to face something larger than themselves.

Then, decades later, a stranger who was wandering in out of the rain looking for nothing more than an escape from the weather, saw a ruin instead of a temple, and turned it into something about sight instead, an opening built to let light through and hold a single image still.

The star hasn’t really disappeared, not technically. It’s just folded into the shape of the lens now, hiding in plain sight, waiting for anyone who knows enough to actually look for it.

Enso

In Zen Buddhist practice, the circle, or enso, is painted in a single unbroken brushstroke, in one continuous motion, without the brush ever lifting or correcting itself. It’s often left slightly open, imperfect, unclosed, and that gap isn’t a mistake, it’s part of the meaning.

The enso represents the universe, emptiness, and enlightenment all at once, but it’s less a symbol to decode than a direct trace of the artist’s state of mind in that exact moment, unplanned, unrepeatable.

Zen practitioners often paint hundreds of ensos over a lifetime, sometimes they paint daily, as a kind of meditative practice rather than an artistic one. No two are ever quite the same, and no enso is considered more “correct” or “better” than another.

“In the beginner’s mind there are many possibilities, but in the expert’s mind there are few.” Shunryu Suzuki

Each enso is painted, in a sense, by a beginner, one continuous, unrepeatable attempt, with no chance to go back and fix it.

IHS Roundel

Long before it appeared on this brick wall, this symbol began as a secret. Early Christians facing persecution used an earlier version, the chi-rho, to identify one another quietly, tracing it into catacomb walls as far back as the 150s AD. Everything changed in 312 AD, when the Roman emperor Constantine reportedly saw the symbol in a vision before battle, accompanied by the words “in this sign, conquer.” He won, and within months, Christianity went from persecuted faith to legal religion across the empire.

The version carved here, IHS, comes from a later evolution, the first three letters of “Jesus” in Greek, later reinterpreted in Latin as Iesus Hominum Salvator, “Jesus, Savior of Mankind.” It spread widely through the Jesuits, who adopted it as their emblem, carrying it into churches like this one, centuries and continents removed from a Roman battlefield.

“In this sign, conquer.”

What’s carved into this roundel isn’t decoration. It’s a name held inside a frame, a circle drawn specifically to mark this one small piece of wall as sacred.
